# Chapter XII - VisaPass

Dora clunked her bike against another haphazardly parked bike in front of the façade of the Municipal Council building of Schoten. The façade had arrows pointing upward shoddily painted on the eighteenth century brickwork designed to make you crane your neck and gaze upward where you could admire the nasal cavities of the Dear Leader of the Free Economic Party, Dustin. Dora shivered, not only from the cold.

Once in the building, where the tapestries were mouldy and the lino on the waiting benches was delapidated, people huddled close together with their dockets clenched in their creased fists. The pervasive musty smell of unwashed bodies and ill-heated houses hit Dora like a physical obstacle. She took a sharp left to the bathroom stalls, holding back an urge to run or to puke. 

With trembling hands she turned opened the faucet and 99.9% detoxified water gurgled out in a thin trickle. Luckily she was alone in the washroom. Apprehensively she put her hands under the thin trail of water and rubbed them against each other. There was no soap to be had, and she regarded her hands, yellowing, crusty and with permanently dark-rimmed nails, as if for the first time. She tried to remember if her hands had ever looked clean in her childhood. Pinkish? Like synth-meat? No, her memory would be wrong…

The radio was on, broadcasting AI-drivel that was not mind-numbing enough to put you to sleep nor too tittilating - just jarring enough for the nerves to keep one on edge. Dora hoped vainly it was not by design.

The radio blathered on: "The cost of intelligence is continuing to decline, expect massive returns on hiring cognitariats; retraining programs available now for the gifted; protein farmer tank clearers wanted in Pampus! Deschool now to become a hedge cleaver, a sedge hopper…"

The running water abruptly stopped with a creak in the ducts and she waved her now also cold hands in the room, the towels no longer being dispensed because of the futile cost. For it had been calculated by the Free Economic Party that people wasted too much time drying their hands.

She abruptly turned awat before the faucet started beeping; an reprimand for her to get a move on and do something to further the needs of the economy.

All the better; she needed to fill in the dozen or so forms required of her to get the coveted, yet hated Visapass. She took a deep breath and re-entered the central hall. 



In the central hall, computer screens or terminals were shaped in pentagonal forms, so you could half see what your neighbour was up to. There were no cats, so curiosity couldn’t kill them in Dora’s age. Purely to assist one nabuur, not for spying at all. The monochrome terminal’s cursor blinked mutely. A yellowing cardboard taped to the right of the terminal listed the instructions and the forms needed for each request or change in one’s life.

 ``` VISAPASS RENEWAL: FORMS 23b12, 5, 12c, BC, old pass, 5M6… ``` 

Under the desk there were pigeonholes for all the forms. You had to insert them into the slot under the monitor after you entered your name, social-fiscal ID and date of birth.

Muttering the numbers to herself, she crouched with the list in hand that she pulled off the tape. "Let’s see, 23b12… where you at…" For all the advantages in technology, the ability to sort alphabetically or in ascending numerical order had passed the municipal data/forms dept by. She entered her forms one by one, entering and pressing F3 in a cabalistic fashion.
Halfway through the ordeal, she entered the BC form before 12c and had to start over. The terminal jammed and beeped. People looked up exasperatedly; this meant longer waiting times for them.

The screen errored, blinking “ERROR”. The sweat of being caught out rivuleted down her spine; she was hot as hell and cold as ice at the same time. Trembling, she looked at the punch-cards, trying to decipher how to fill it in properly. It was supposed to be a breeze, Tako had said. He’d probably never had to fill in one of those awful things.

A voice in her ear said, “Allow me, may I?” — a soft, creaky voice like an old vinyl recording of a nineteen-sixties violin concert — Beethoven. Dora looked up in fear and desperation. It was the kind visage of Raymond, neighbour, boomer extraordinaire.

“A renewal, is it?” he muttered, and shuffled the half a dozen stack of punch-cards. “12c is the one we want,” he said, and blacked out a number of the lozenges, as well as adding a bunch on the side. 

“And now for the tricky bit,” he said, and procured a bit of sellotape stuck on his visacard. He used a plastic spudger and deftly covered three lozenges in the middle somewhere. 

“There, that should foil the cross-checking of ‘12B’ with that of your birth certificate. Let me peruse the others… yes, looks alright, let’s check 5… here we go, skip the subroutine here,” and he sellotaped some more. “Dora, just have a look around to see we’re not being Eagled…”

Dora peeked over the consoles to the counters where the civil servants were processing the vast stacks of punch-cards. A male civilian, hunched in an overcoat a few sizes too large, was whining petulantly; you could hear him over the whirring of the clunking machines and the punching and the sneezing and shuffling of the waiting mob. Other Eagles looked over at his counter, disturbed. 

Suddenly his large overcoat bulged and out sprung a little jockey dog, a Jack Russell by the looks of it. Yapping loudly it whizzed around the chamber, its owner in full pursuit, coattails flapping, whining and yelling. The citizens were dashing away, afraid to get caught up in the hullabaloo.

“Now’s our chance,” said Raymond, and entered the forms one by one, the screen giving a “CLEARED” and spitting out a receipt at the end, a ticker tape punched free in a riotously optimistic yellow. “Go get your visapass, Dora, now,” and he gave her his docket. “Counter 5, go!” and the boomer pushed her in the small of her back so that she almost fell over.

She stumbled to the counter and slid the two emblems of her freedom under the plexiglass shield. “Here you are, ma’am, a request is authorised.”

She glanced into the chaos of the running dog, who was having the time of its life slaloming around the security guards. She noticed Raymond was egging on the dog even more. The civil servant was clearly agitated, and when the new visapass was spat out she didn’t give Dora any scrutinous look. She whisked it away, turned, and saw security had bodyslammed the dog owner, and it looked like they had chucked a blanket over the little dog, who was now all caught up in it and disorientated.
